c01s03 Chapter 1: Development includes gains and constraints.
Development: change and continuity across life. Plasticity: capacity for change under conditions. Context: social and historical circumstances.

Developmental psychology studies patterns of change and continuity from before birth through later life. Gains in one skill can accompany costs in another.

Jo and Pat differ in experience as well as age. The café episode does not identify an age effect or either person’s general ability.

c01s03b Chapter 1: Age groups are not a time machine.
Cross-sectional study: compares age groups at one time. Cohort: people sharing a historical period.

A cross-sectional comparison can reveal age differences, but cohorts may differ in schooling or technology exposure. A longitudinal study can examine within-person change.

Longitudinal findings can still reflect repeated testing and selective dropout. Combining designs helps distinguish age, history and measurement effects.

c01s04 Chapter 1: Describe the task before the person.
Measurement: a defined way of recording an outcome. Practice effect: change from taking a task again. Attrition: loss of participants over time.

Record learning accuracy, time and prior experience on comparable tasks. Follow change rather than ranking the worth of age groups.

An accessible display may help Pat immediately without changing an underlying ability. Developmental explanations need evidence about the task, the environment and the person over time.

c01s05 Chapter 1: What does the evidence allow?
Lifespan research treats development as multidirectional and embedded in historical contexts. Average age trends summarize samples; they do not fix an individual’s trajectory.

Selective samples, repeated testing and changing environments limit causal claims. The fictional café comparison illustrates those limits; it is not developmental data.

c02s03 Chapter 2: Development starts before birth.
Gestation: the course of pregnancy. Teratogen: an exposure that can disrupt prenatal development.

Prenatal development involves changing biological systems. Some exposures can increase the risk of harm, and effects depend partly on when development is occurring.

This does not mean an exposure guarantees an outcome. Advice about a specific pregnancy belongs with a qualified healthcare professional who can evaluate the actual exposure.

c02s03b Chapter 2: Timing changes the question.
Sensitive period: time of heightened responsiveness. Risk factor: condition associated with increased likelihood.

A developing system can be more responsive to an exposure during particular periods. Dose, duration, nutrition, health and other exposures may also matter.

A population association does not show which factor caused a particular child’s difficulty. Retrospective accounts can leave important details unmeasured.

c02s04 Chapter 2: Support changes a developmental pathway.
Protective factor: condition that can reduce adverse risk. Probabilistic: concerning likelihood rather than certainty. Developmental pathway: pattern of change over time.

Healthcare access and supportive environments can influence later opportunities. Biological and social development continue to interact after birth.

Avoid assigning blame from a simplified origin story. Ask what is known now and which practical supports are appropriate, without promising that support reverses every possible effect.

c02s05 Chapter 2: What does the evidence allow?
Prenatal risk evidence often comes from observational studies because harmful exposures cannot ethically be assigned. Confounding, exposure measurement and differences between outcomes matter.

A sensitive period is specific to a system and experience. It does not mean that all development ends after an early deadline.

c03s03 Chapter 3: Infants provide indirect evidence.
Habituation: reduced responding with repeated stimulation. Dishabituation: renewed responding after a change. Looking time: duration of visual attention.

Infants cannot explain an answer verbally. Researchers therefore use responses such as looking, reaching or sucking under defined conditions.

A renewed look can indicate discrimination or novelty. It does not by itself reveal a complete adult-like concept. Visual salience and familiarity are alternative explanations to test.

c03s03b Chapter 3: Different tasks ask different questions.
Object permanence: understanding that objects continue unseen. Task demand: what successful performance requires.

Searching for a hidden object requires remembering a location and coordinating action. A looking task has different demands.

Different ages of success across tasks need not mean one method is useless. Compare the specific ability each task requires and seek converging evidence before making a broad claim.

c03s04 Chapter 3: Early learning is active and situated.
Joint attention: sharing attention toward something. Contingent response: response related to a preceding signal. Individual variation: differences among developmental paths.

Infants learn through perception, movement and interaction with caregivers. Shared attention can support learning about objects and words.

Developmental milestones describe useful patterns of change, with variation. A casual observation is not a developmental assessment. Persistent concerns are a reason to seek appropriate professional advice.

c03s05 Chapter 3: What does the evidence allow?
Infant methods provide valuable evidence when comparison events control low-level differences and results replicate. Different measures can produce different developmental estimates.

The fictional toy event does not demonstrate a universal timetable, intelligence score or diagnosis.

c04s03 Chapter 4: Thinking changes through activity.
Schema: organized understanding of a situation. Assimilation: interpreting experience using an existing schema. Accommodation: changing a schema to fit experience.

Piaget described children as actively constructing understanding. New experiences can fit an existing way of thinking or require revising it.

His broad stages remain historically influential, but performance also varies with task wording, experience and support. Do not use a stage label as a complete account of a particular child.

c04s03b Chapter 4: Help can reveal a learning opportunity.
Zone of proximal development: gap between independent and supported performance. Scaffolding: temporary support adjusted to a task. Guided participation: learning through supported shared activity.

Vygotsky emphasized learning in social and cultural activity. Success with guidance identifies what is possible with that support.

Jo can model one comparison, invite the visitor to explain, then reduce help. Independent performance needs a later attempt without the prompt and a suitable new example.

c04s04 Chapter 4: Language grows in interaction.
Receptive language: understanding communicated language. Expressive language: producing communicated language. Bilingual development: development involving more than one language.

Understanding and producing words are different demands. Vocabulary in each language depends on exposure and context; a single-language count can miss knowledge expressed elsewhere.

Invite clear, responsive communication rather than judging ability from one conversation. Concerns about development require culturally and linguistically appropriate assessment.

c04s05 Chapter 4: What does the evidence allow?
Developmental theories guide questions rather than establish rigid instructions for every age. Evidence on guided learning depends on how assistance and later transfer are measured.

The café task is an illustration. It does not validate a teaching method for all learners or make a developmental assessment.

c05s03 Chapter 5: Closeness can support exploration.
Secure base: a caregiver relationship supporting exploration. Safe haven: comfort sought when distressed.

Attachment theory concerns how relationships support safety and exploration, particularly under stress. Staying close in an unfamiliar room can have several explanations.

A short café visit cannot classify a child or caregiver. Researchers use structured procedures and consider the relationship and context being measured.

c05s03b Chapter 5: A pattern is not an identity.
Attachment pattern: observed relationship-related organization. Caregiving sensitivity: noticing and responding appropriately to signals.

Temperament and attachment address different questions. A child may react strongly to noise without having an insecure relationship.

Attachment classifications summarize behavior in specified circumstances. They are not moral ratings, permanent personality types or diagnoses from a single behavior.

c05s04 Chapter 5: Relationships develop in wider conditions.
Responsiveness: appropriate response to another’s signals. Continuity: persistence across time. Change: variation in later relationship experience.

Responsive care matters, but caregiving occurs within work, health, housing and cultural conditions. Children can form attachments with more than one caregiver.

Early patterns can be associated with later outcomes without determining them. Later relationships and changing circumstances also contribute to development.

c05s05 Chapter 5: What does the evidence allow?
Attachment evidence uses relationship-specific measures and longitudinal comparisons. Cultural practices, measurement and stress conditions affect interpretation.

Early associations with later outcomes are probabilistic. They do not show that one behavior identifies a permanent attachment style or parental fault.

c06s03 Chapter 6: Development has connected settings.
Microsystem: immediate setting and relationships. Mesosystem: connections between immediate settings.

An ecological approach examines the child in family, school, peer and community settings. Links between those settings can influence experience.

A quiet response at the café may reflect unfamiliar language, relationships or task expectations. A parenting explanation is one possible account, not a finding established by the observation.

c06s03b Chapter 6: Influences can run both ways.
Bidirectional influence: each participant affects the other. Selection effect: people enter different environments. Confounding: another factor contributes to an association.

Children affect adult responses while adults shape children’s opportunities. Family stress, resources and individual characteristics may influence both.

A correlation between a parenting measure and behavior does not isolate a one-way cause. Stronger designs examine alternative explanations and change over time.

c06s04 Chapter 6: Peers can support different behaviors.
Peer norm: expectation within a peer group. Socialization: learning shared practices and expectations. Exosystem: settings affecting a child indirectly.

Peers can encourage participation, practice and belonging, as well as pressure toward risky actions. The effect depends on the group and situation.

An adult’s work schedule may change a child’s available support indirectly. Examine specific conditions before turning a contextual pattern into a judgment about a family.

c06s05 Chapter 6: What does the evidence allow?
Ecological models organize possible influences; drawing a map does not prove every proposed causal arrow. Observational associations need careful interpretation.

Culture, resources and structural constraints qualify comparisons of family practices. One preferred family style is not a universal explanation of outcomes.

Education, work, partnership and caring roles occur in different orders and at different times. Norms about timing depend on social conditions and culture.

c10s03 Chapter 10: Connect time with context.
Continuity: persistence in a developmental pattern. Discontinuity: a qualitative change in a pattern. Transactional account: person and environment influence each other over time.

A developmental explanation asks what changed, for whom, in which setting and over what period. Stable patterns can coexist with change in particular skills.

The same workshop outcome can reflect prior experience, access, relationships and instruction. Explain the proposed mechanism instead of substituting an age label.

c10s03b Chapter 10: Evaluate support against an outcome.
Intervention: planned change intended to affect an outcome. Transfer: use in a different situation. Follow-up: measurement after time has passed.

Choose a support that addresses a specific demand, such as a clear example or accessible instructions. Define the intended outcome before judging success.

Compare comparable conditions, check an unfamiliar example and revisit later. Immediate participation is useful evidence, but it does not establish durable independent learning.

c10s04 Chapter 10: Use knowledge without claiming authority over people.
Consent: freely given agreement to participation. Privacy: control over personal information. Proportionate inference: conclusion limited to the evidence.

Developmental ideas can make instructions more accessible and interpretations more careful. They do not authorize informal diagnosis, surveillance or experiments on children.

Use fictional cases or your own low-stakes tasks for practice. For genuine developmental or health concerns, seek relevant professional advice rather than assigning a label.

c10s05 Chapter 10: What does the evidence allow?
A developmental model earns support through suitable evidence, not simply by fitting a life story after the event. Effects may vary across populations and settings.

Course completion and self-confidence are not evidence of professional competence. An unfamiliar later case provides a more useful learning check.
